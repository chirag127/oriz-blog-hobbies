Film is having a real moment, and that means two things: it has never been easier to find a camera, and it has never been easier to overpay for one. You do not need a Leica, a light meter app, or a $400 point-and-shoot that went viral. You need a working camera, one roll of forgiving film, and a lab. Here is the honest path.

## Buy a used SLR, not a trendy point-and-shoot

The internet pushed the price of compact point-and-shoots (the Contax T2, the Olympus mju) into absurd territory — often $300 to $600 for a plastic camera with a fixed lens and no repair path. Skip that entire category.

Instead, buy a used 35mm SLR from the 1980s. The Canon AE-1, Pentax K1000, Nikon FM, and Minolta X-700 were made in the millions, they are mechanical and repairable, and a tested body with a 50mm lens runs $80 to $150. The 50mm f/1.8 "nifty fifty" that comes with most of them is genuinely sharp and shoots well in low light.

What to check before buying:

- **Light seals.** The foam around the door rots. A $10 seal kit fixes it, but negotiate the price down.
- **Meter.** Load a battery and check the needle or LEDs respond to light. A dead meter is not fatal (you can use the [Sunny 16 rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunny_16_rule)) but it should be priced accordingly.
- **Shutter.** Fire it at every speed. The slow speeds (1s, 1/2s) should sound different from the fast ones, not stick.

## The one film to start with

Buy **Kodak Gold 200** or **Kodak ColorPlus 200**. Both are cheap, forgiving of exposure mistakes, and give the warm, slightly nostalgic look people actually associate with film. Shoot color negative before you touch slide film or black and white — negative film has the widest exposure latitude, meaning you can be a stop or two off and still get a usable frame.

Skip these until later: Portra (expensive, wasted on learning), any expired film (a variable you do not need yet), and slide film like Velvia (beautiful, unforgiving, and costly to develop).

## Developing: do not do it yourself yet

Home developing is a great hobby-within-the-hobby, but starting there adds chemistry, temperature control, and a changing bag to a learning curve that is already steep. Find a local lab or use a mail-in service. Ask for **"develop and scan"** — you get your negatives back plus digital files you can actually share.

A useful budget line: a roll of 36 exposures costs roughly $8 to $12 for the film and another $12 to $18 to develop and scan. That is real money per frame, and it is the single best thing about film for a beginner — it forces you to slow down and think before you press the shutter.

## The three mistakes that cost the most

1. **Buying gear instead of shooting.** One body, one lens, one film. Shoot ten rolls before you buy anything else. Most "I need a better camera" feelings are actually "I need more practice."
2. **Ignoring the light.** Film has less dynamic range than your phone. Shoot with the sun behind you or to the side, avoid harsh midday contrast, and treat golden hour as a gift.
3. **Chimping habits.** There is no screen to check. Note your settings for the first few rolls, then compare against the scans. That feedback loop is how you learn exposure faster than any tutorial.

## A realistic first kit

- Canon AE-1 or Pentax K1000 body with 50mm lens — $120
- Two rolls of Kodak Gold 200 — $20
- Fresh battery for the meter — $6
- A lab you trust — priceless

That is under $150 to genuinely start, and every piece of it holds its value. Shoot a few rolls, learn what you actually enjoy photographing, and let the next purchase be driven by a real limitation you have hit — not by a video that made a camera look cool.

The best camera for getting into film is a cheap, working one in your hands this weekend. Everything else is a distraction dressed up as a requirement.
