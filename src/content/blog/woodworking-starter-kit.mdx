Most woodworking starter advice tells you to buy a table saw, a router, and a garage full of machines. That advice sells tools and stops beginners cold. You can build real, useful, good-looking things with a compact set of hand tools on a kitchen table — and you will learn more doing it. Here is the kit, in the order it matters.

## Buy these first

**Measuring and marking**

- A **combination square** (6-inch). This is the tool you reach for most: marking square lines, checking that a cut is 90 degrees, setting depth. Buy one decent one; a wobbly square makes everything downstream wrong.
- A **marking knife** or a sharp pencil. A knife line is more precise than pencil and gives your saw a groove to start in.
- A **tape measure** you already own.

**Cutting**

- A **Japanese pull saw** (a ryoba or dozuki). It cuts on the pull stroke, which keeps the thin blade from bending, and it is far more forgiving for a beginner than a Western push saw. One of these does more than a $600 miter saw for your first year.

**Joining and shaping**

- A **set of three chisels** (1/4", 1/2", 3/4"). For cleaning joints, paring, and light mortising. They must be sharp — see below.
- A **block plane**. For truing edges, easing sharp corners, and fixing a cut that came out proud.

**Holding**

- Two **clamps**, or a cheap **portable workbench** with a built-in vise. You cannot cut or chisel wood that is sliding around. This is the step beginners skip and then wonder why their joints are sloppy.

That whole list is roughly $120 to $180 and fits in a drawer.

## Sharpening is the skill, not a chore

Here is the thing nobody says loudly enough: **dull tools are why beginners quit.** A dull chisel crushes wood fibers instead of slicing them, tears out grain, and needs dangerous force. A sharp one glides.

Buy a **cheap sharpening setup** — a double-sided diamond plate or a couple of sharpening stones and an inexpensive honing guide — for about $40. Learn to put a mirror edge on your chisels and plane iron. Spend an evening on it before your first project. Sharpening is not the boring part of woodworking; it is the part that makes the rest possible.

## Why skip the power tools

Power tools are faster, and speed is exactly what a beginner does not need. Hand tools:

- Are quiet and dust-light enough to use in an apartment.
- Fail gently — a hand saw wandering off the line teaches you; a table saw kickback hurts you.
- Force you to understand the wood: grain direction, how it splits, how a joint actually fits.
- Cost a fraction of the machines and take a fraction of the space.

You can always add a drill (the one power tool genuinely worth buying early) and, much later, a bench saw. But learn to cut a straight line and pare a clean joint by hand first. Those skills make you better with machines, not the other way around.

## Your first project: a small box or a bench hook

Do not build a bookshelf. Build a **bench hook** — a simple jig that hooks over your table edge and gives you a square surface to saw against. It uses a saw, a chisel, a square, and glue; it takes an afternoon; and it is a tool you will use in every project after. It is the woodworking equivalent of a program that prints "hello, world" and then turns out to be genuinely useful.

If you want something to keep, a **small hardwood box** with simple butt joints, sanded and finished with a wipe-on oil, teaches measuring, square cutting, gluing, and finishing in one project.

## Finishing without the rabbit hole

Finishing has as many opinions as switches do keycaps. Ignore them. Sand to 220 grit, wipe on a **hardwax oil** or plain boiled linseed oil, let it cure, done. It looks good, it is repairable, and you can learn spray lacquer another decade.

## The realistic starter kit

- Combination square, marking knife, pull saw — $60
- Three chisels + block plane — $70
- Diamond plate + honing guide — $40
- Two clamps — $25
- A can of hardwax oil — $15

That is roughly $210 for everything, and it is a kit you will still be using in ten years — the good chisels only get better as you learn to sharpen them. Build the bench hook this weekend, keep your tools sharp, and let the projects get bigger as your hands get surer. The workshop grows from skill, not from the checkout page.
