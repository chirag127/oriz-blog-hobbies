The mechanical keyboard hobby has a reputation for being expensive and confusing, and both are avoidable. The confusion comes from jargon; the expense comes from buying twice. This guide fixes the first so you avoid the second. By the end you should be able to read a product page and know exactly what you are getting.

## Start with the switch, because it is what you feel

Every keypress runs through a switch, and switches come in three families:

- **Linear** (usually red): smooth top to bottom, no bump, no click. Quiet-ish, good for fast typing and gaming. The safe default if you are unsure.
- **Tactile** (usually brown): a small bump partway down tells you the key registered. Most people who type all day end up here.
- **Clicky** (usually blue): tactile plus an audible click. Satisfying, and hated by everyone who shares a room with you.

You cannot learn what you like from a chart. Buy a **switch tester** — a little block with 9 to 12 switches on it for about $10 — and press them for a day before committing. This single purchase saves the most money in the whole hobby.

## Get a hot-swap board so a mistake is not permanent

The most important spec on your first keyboard is **hot-swappable**. It means switches press into sockets instead of being soldered in. If you buy a board with linear switches and decide you want tactile, you pull them out and push new ones in — no soldering iron, no regret.

Almost every good entry board is hot-swap now. If a listing does not say "hot-swap," assume it is soldered and move on.

## Layout: how many keys, and where

Layout is about how much desk you want the board to eat:

- **Full-size (100%):** everything, including the number pad. Big.
- **TKL (tenkeyless, 80%):** drops the number pad. The most popular "does everything" size.
- **75%:** TKL keys squeezed together with arrow keys intact. Compact without losing much.
- **65%:** drops the function row but keeps arrows. Small, still practical.
- **60%:** no function row, no arrows (they move to a layer). Smallest common size; a real adjustment.

For a first board, **75% or TKL** is the sweet spot: compact enough to feel like the hobby, complete enough that nothing you rely on has moved.

## The parts you can ignore for now

Keyboard content is full of upgrades that matter to enthusiasts and not to you yet:

- **Keycap profiles** (Cherry, SA, OEM): affects feel and looks. The stock caps are fine to start.
- **Plate material, foam mods, tape mod, lubing switches:** all real, all optional, all things you do *after* you know you are staying.
- **Gasket mount vs tray mount:** a sound and flex difference most beginners cannot hear. Skip the debate.

Buying a board because it has a "gasket-mounted polycarbonate plate" before you have typed on anything is exactly the buy-twice trap.

## A spec-checklist for the product page

Before you buy, confirm the listing says:

1. **Hot-swappable** (non-negotiable)
2. **Wired, or wireless with a wired fallback** (batteries die mid-deadline)
3. **Standard bottom row** (so keycap sets fit later)
4. **QMK/VIA support** if you want to remap keys yourself
5. **TKL or 75%** unless you have a specific reason otherwise

## A realistic first setup

- A hot-swap 75% barebones board — $50 to $90
- A switch tester, then a full set of your chosen switch — $10 + $25
- A decent PBT keycap set (optional, but a big feel upgrade) — $30

That lands around $90 to $150 for a board you assembled yourself, understand completely, and can modify without buying a new one. Compare that to a $60 sealed board you will replace in six weeks when you decide reds were the wrong call.

The hobby rewards patience in one specific way: test switches first, buy hot-swap, keep the layout familiar. Do those three things and your first keyboard becomes the one you actually keep — and the platform for every fun, unnecessary upgrade that comes after.
